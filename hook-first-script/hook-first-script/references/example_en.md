# Worked example: "How to fall asleep faster" (3–5 min)

This is a full run of the skill. Answers the user gave in step 0:

1. Topic: how to fall asleep faster. Takeaway: "Four small changes to the hour before bed that make it easier to fall asleep."
2. Audience: adults who lie awake for a while most nights; no sleep-science background.
3. Length: about 4 minutes (≈ 560–640 words at 140–160 wpm).
4. Tone: calm explainer, a little warm. English, casual register.
5. Sources: none provided. So the script sticks to widely known sleep-hygiene advice and lists every factual claim for checking.

## Title drafts
1. Can't Fall Asleep? Change These 4 Things Before Bed
2. The Hour Before Bed Decides How Fast You Fall Asleep
3. Stop Trying Harder to Sleep (Do This Instead)

## Hook options
- A. **Question**: "What's the last thing you looked at before you tried to sleep last night?"
- B. **Surprising fact**: "Trying harder to fall asleep is one of the things that keeps you awake."
- C. **Scene**: "It's 1 a.m. You've checked the time three times. Every time, it's worse."

**Recommended: B.** It runs against what viewers expect, it's true to the video's last part, and it sets up the open loop for Part 4. Use C's image as the opening shot.

## Script

**00:00 Hook**
Trying harder to fall asleep is one of the things that keeps you awake.
Sounds backwards, right? Stay with me.

**00:10 Promise**
In this video, you'll get four small changes to the hour before bed. None of them cost anything.

**00:20 Part 1 – Same wake-up time** *(shot note: alarm clock at the same time on a calendar, Monday to Sunday)*
Let's start with the morning, not the night.
Your body gets sleepy on a schedule. If you wake up at a different time every day, that schedule drifts.
So pick one wake-up time and keep it, even on weekends.
Sounds simple. But there's one thing that undoes it every evening, and it's probably in your hand right now.

**01:00 Part 2 – Dim the lights, park the phone** *(shot note: room lights dimming; phone placed face down on a shelf outside the bedroom)*
Bright light in the evening tells your brain it's still daytime.
That includes ceiling lights, and yes, your phone.
About an hour before bed, switch to warm, low lamps.
And give your phone a place to sleep that isn't your pillow. A shelf, the kitchen, anywhere.
If you use it as an alarm, a cheap alarm clock does the same job.
Now your eyes are ready. Your room might not be.

**01:50 Part 3 – Cool, dark, quiet** *(shot note: hand adjusting a thermostat; blackout curtain closing)*
A cool room usually helps. Most people sleep better when it's slightly cooler than daytime comfort.
Block out light if you can. Curtains or a simple eye mask both work.
If noise wakes you, a steady sound like a fan can cover it.
And caffeine? Keep it to the morning. It lasts much longer than the buzz you feel.
You've done everything right. And you're still awake. Here's where most people make it worse.

**02:50 Part 4 – Stop trying** *(shot note: person getting out of bed, reading a paper book under a small lamp)*
Remember the start? Trying harder keeps you awake.
When you lie there forcing sleep, your bed turns into the place where you worry.
So if you've been awake for what feels like a while, get up.
Do something calm and boring in dim light. Fold laundry. Read a paper book.
Go back when you feel sleepy. Not before.
Don't watch the clock. Turn it around if you have to.

**03:40 Recap**
Quick recap.
One: same wake-up time every day.
Two: dim lights and park the phone an hour before bed.
Three: cool, dark, quiet room. Caffeine in the morning only.
And if sleep won't come, get up instead of fighting it.

**04:00 Next step**
If this helped, subscribe for more small habits that actually stick.
Next up: a five-minute morning routine that makes tonight's sleep easier.

## Fact-check list
- "Your body gets sleepy on a schedule" / a fixed wake time helps: verify with a sleep-medicine source (e.g. a national sleep foundation or health service page).
- Evening bright light and screens delay sleepiness: verify; avoid quoting specific wavelengths or percentages unless sourced.
- "Slightly cooler room helps": verify; do not state a specific temperature unless a source gives one.
- Caffeine "lasts much longer than the buzz": verify the typical duration before giving any number of hours.
- "Get up if you can't sleep" (stimulus control): verify it is standard advice from a medical source.
- Add a one-line note on screen: "If sleep problems last for weeks, talk to a doctor." (The video gives general tips, not medical advice.)
